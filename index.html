Prologue

Metal from the Sky

Hand-polished WWII aircraft aluminum · USAAF Bombardier Badge

A thick, hand-formed cuff cut from World War II aircraft aluminum. At its center: the striking, outspread wings of a United States Army Air Forces Bombardier — a downward-facing bomb between those wings, contoured and dual-riveted to the band.

This is not costume jewelry. It is trench art. Scrap from the machines that kept young men alive, turned into a token for the woman who kept one of them human.

Newman served in the Navy as an Aviation Radioman and Ordnance Specialist. Archival research reveals he went out of his way to acquire these specific wings — evidently bartering on base for their more distinguished design over standard naval issue.

He made it during his wartime service and gave it to Joan “Joannie” Gloeckler — high-school and college sweetheart, the girl who knew the boy before the blue eyes became legend.

Act I

Before the Blue Eyes
Were Famous

Shaker Heights. Denison. A large grey Chrysler his father called safe. Dates at a deli on Chagrin Boulevard. The ordinary years that made the legend possible.

Budin’s, Chaperones & Boogie-Woogie

Romance lived at Budin’s Delicatessen — 17133 Chagrin Boulevard. The neon, the booths, the exact doorway still visible in the photographs she kept.

She had to bring her little brother John as chaperone. They stashed him under the stairs.

And there, in the back, Paul played boogie-woogie piano — loud, private, joyful. A talent almost no one later knew he had.

When he left for the Navy she declined his fraternity pin. Decades later she laughed: “I was already keeping too many others. (That was mean.)”

Body and Soul — “Bumckus”

The inscribed Navy portrait he gave her. White uniform. Palm trees. His handwriting across the bottom:

“Body and Soul – ‘Bumckus’”

A private sign-off. A nickname. Proof that the future icon once wrote only for her.

While he was gone she helped his mother pack care packages. Popcorn as insulation. Wax paper around the cake. The Gloeckler family maintained a notable multi-generational passion for popcorn. Joan proudly noted that her mother and family “taught him everything he knows about popcorn.” The methods behind his future culinary legacy were anchored in the Gloeckler family kitchen.

The Boy in Jacksonville

One of the very few surviving photographs of Paul from the years that matter most to this story. Mounted in her scrapbook with care and labeled in her hand:

“Paul Newman (Phi Tau)
Jax., Florida / 1944”

Not the movie star. Not the icon. Just the young sailor she knew — the one who played boogie-woogie, drove the grey Chrysler, and shaped sky-metal with his own hands.

The handwriting is believed to be Joan’s, not Newman’s. His hand is the portrait inscription. The physical scrapbook page has not been located; only this photograph of it remains.

Joan “Joannie” Gloeckler King

1926 – 2021

Joan “Joannie” Gloeckler King (1926 – 2021) was an force of nature—full of joy, elegance, and a wonderfully sharp sense of humor. Shaker Heights High School, June 7, 1944. Denison University — art major, Delta Delta Delta, voted the campus "Sweetheart of Sigma Chi." Cleveland Institute of Art.

Forty years a board-certified interior designer (ASID). Painter in watercolor, oil, gouache. Worked on site in Monet’s garden at Giverny, the Cotswolds, Paris, Venice.

Lifelong golfer. Country Club of Pepper Pike. Wilderness Country Club, Naples. She divided her time between her beloved residence of 25 years in Ohio, and later Naples, Florida. Convertible driver into her final years.

She kept the pulse of fashion, parties, and life itself until 95. And she kept his wartime token when every rule of her era said she was supposed to destroy it.

Act III

The Silence That
Made It Sacred

After the war a magazine profile of Newman mentioned a life-altering “Dear John” letter. In the archive sits the clipping. In her own hand she wrote:

“The ‘Dear John’ letter that I wrote to Paul is mentioned in this magazine.”

The Archival Gap Analysis (The Missing Letters)

Upon her formal marriage in October 1948, Joan destroyed the extensive personal letters written to her by Paul Newman during their courtship, as was the custom of that time. While this concludes a standard paper trail, it establishes an authentic historical explanation for the absence of correspondence and exponentially increases the historical rarity and value of the surviving artifacts. These remaining physical assets represent the last surviving material footprints of this foundational relationship.

On October 2, 1948, Joan Lynn Gloeckler married her first husband, Randall Duane Fulmer, at the First Baptist Church in Greater Cleveland, with a formal reception following at a private local club. In an extraordinary testament to the enduring familial and personal bond between the sweethearts, a young Paul Newman—then a student at Kenyon College—traveled to Cleveland to attend the wedding in person. As a profound, bittersweet gesture marking the formal conclusion of their romantic chapter, Newman gifted the newlyweds a custom, engraved silver serving tray.

That custom is why the paper trail ends. That custom is why photographs of the two of them together do not exist. That custom is why what she chose to keep is almost holy.

She was supposed to destroy this cuff.
She did not.

No photograph of them together survived.
The metal did.

The Tea Room Reunion

Decades after the war, Joan visited Mrs. Newman for tea after noticing Paul's face on collegiate dorm posters while touring schools with her daughters. During the visit, Mrs. Newman gifted the family signed photographs of Paul and wistfully asked, “Oh Joannie, aren't you sorry?” to which Joan replied, “No, no, I'm happily married.”

Decades later — the whisper

In an exclusive Los Angeles designer showroom, Joannie walked up behind Newman (standing with Joanne Woodward) and said quietly, “Guess who’s standing next to you?” The legend flushed. The wartime objects had already outlived every rule that tried to erase them.

Act IV

What She Kept

The cuff. The inscribed portrait. The scrapbook. The diploma. The annotations in her own hand. The last material witnesses to a story fame almost erased.

Act V

Why This Object
Matters

Pre-fame intimacy

Newman memorabilia is vast. Personal wartime tokens given to a named high-school sweetheart — with matching inscription, scrapbook, and her own handwriting — are vanishingly rare.

The survival itself

She was supposed to destroy it. The culture of 1948 demanded the earlier romance be erased. That she kept the cuff, the portrait, and the annotations is the entire point.

A Unique Biographical Connection

Hollywood legend, World War II Navy service, Shaker Heights upbringing, and collegiate proximity all run through the history of an elegant family that shared its kitchen traditions with a future icon.
